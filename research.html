Research

How viral protein machines move genomes across biological barriers

My group investigates the molecular machines that enable positive-strand RNA viruses to move their genomes across biological barriers. We study viruses of medical and veterinary importance, with the aim of uncovering fundamental principles of viral entry, replication and assembly, and translating these insights into new antiviral strategies.

Throughout their replication cycle, viruses must repeatedly overcome physical barriers. During entry, viral surface proteins undergo precisely controlled conformational changes that allow the genome to cross cellular membranes and reach the appropriate intracellular environment. Once inside the cell, viruses remodel host membranes to create specialised replication compartments, raising a second fundamental problem: how newly synthesised RNA genomes are transported from these protected environments for translation and packaging.

We seek to understand the protein machinery that controls these movements. Our work therefore focuses on the structure, dynamics and mechanism of viral proteins, from receptor-activated entry proteins such as coronavirus spike to ATP-driven molecular motors involved in RNA replication and transport.

To address these questions, we combine cryo-electron microscopy, computational structural biology, biochemistry and molecular virology. By integrating structural snapshots with functional experiments, we aim to understand not simply what viral proteins look like, but how their architecture, assembly and conformational dynamics generate biological function.

Themes

Our current research centres on three interconnected themes

01

Molecular machines controlling virus entry

We investigate how viral attachment proteins recognise cellular receptors and how receptor engagement triggers structural rearrangements that enable entry. A particular interest is understanding how glycan and protein receptors regulate the conformational dynamics of coronavirus spike proteins.

02

Molecular machines controlling viral RNA replication and transport

We study the poorly understood AAA+ ATPases encoded by positive-strand RNA viruses. We aim to determine how these proteins assemble, interact with membranes and RNA, and use ATP hydrolysis to coordinate genome replication, transport and assembly.

03

Targeting viral molecular machines with antivirals

Because these proteins perform essential mechanical steps in the viral life cycle, they also present attractive therapeutic targets. We use mechanistic and structural insights to identify vulnerabilities that can be exploited by small molecules, peptides, antibodies and other biologics.

By understanding how viral protein machines move genomes into cells, through intracellular replication environments and towards assembly into new virus particles, we aim to uncover general principles of positive-strand RNA virus biology and provide a molecular foundation for the development of new antiviral therapies.

We make our structural data openly available to the scientific community through depositions in the Protein Data Bank, the Electron Microscopy Data Bank, and EMPIAR.

Academic Spin-offs
